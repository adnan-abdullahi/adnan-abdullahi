# 1. Stage Purpose

Stage 2 determines what the professional portfolio experience should provide and how visitors should experience, understand, inspect, and evaluate the engineer's work.

The purpose is not merely to determine which pages the portfolio should contain.

The purpose is to establish:

- who uses the portfolio;
- what uncertainty they bring;
- what they need to accomplish;
- what information the portfolio must represent;
- how that information relates;
- how visitors move through it;
- how evidence is discovered and inspected;
- how technical depth is provided progressively;
- how the interface communicates this structure;
- and whether the proposed experience is sufficiently coherent to proceed toward formal specification.

The central question is:

> **How can the portfolio reduce uncertainty about professional engineering capability without replacing evidence with self-presentation?**

---

# 2. Relationship to the Product Constitution

Stage 2 operates under the principles established in Stage 1.

The portfolio must:

- demonstrate rather than merely claim;
- prioritize evidence over assertion;
- preserve human judgment;
- represent uncertainty honestly;
- support progressive depth;
- serve human needs;
- maintain accessibility;
- avoid unnecessary complexity;
- and allow the engineering process itself to become evidence.

Stage 2 therefore treats the portfolio as an **evidence and evaluation system presented through a professional portfolio interface**, rather than as a conventional collection of portfolio pages.

---

# 3. Central Experience Model

The portfolio must support two connected purposes.

## External Purpose

Allow another person to:

> **Discover → Understand → Question → Explore → Verify → Judge → Go Deeper → Judge Again**

## Internal Purpose

Allow the engineer to:

> **Build → Observe → Evaluate → Reflect → Identify Gap → Learn → Build Again**

The portfolio connects these two loops.

The visitor uses the portfolio to evaluate the engineer.

The engineer uses the portfolio to evaluate and develop their own capability and evidence.

---

# 4. Stage 2A — Users, Visitors & Their Uncertainties

## 4.1 Primary Visitor

The primary visitor is an unfamiliar professional evaluator.

The visitor does not begin with complete knowledge of the engineer.

Their fundamental question is:

> **Can I develop enough confidence in this engineer to justify further attention?**

The experience must therefore reduce uncertainty progressively rather than attempting to establish trust through unsupported self-description.

---

## 4.2 Secondary Users

The portfolio must also serve:

- recruiters and professional evaluators;
- technical engineers;
- potential clients or problem owners;
- potential collaborators;
- the engineer themself.

Each group may require a different depth of information.

---

## 4.3 Visitor Depths

The experience supports three broad depths:

### Orientation

The visitor understands:

- who the engineer is;
- what the engineer does;
- why the portfolio exists;
- and where meaningful work can be explored.

### Professional Evidence

The visitor examines:

- projects;
- problems;
- engineering work;
- evidence;
- outcomes;
- capabilities;
- and professional qualities.

### Deep Engineering

The visitor can inspect:

- investigation;
- requirements;
- UX;
- architecture;
- implementation;
- decisions;
- verification;
- technical artifacts;
- failures;
- limitations;
- and reflection.

---

## 4.4 Core Principle

> **Philosophy should be discoverable through the work, not merely stated.**

The interface must therefore allow the engineer's philosophy to emerge from actual decisions, evidence, process, and outcomes.

---

# 5. Stage 2B — User Jobs & Portfolio Jobs

The analysis established that projects themselves are not the fundamental job of the portfolio.

Projects are means of producing evidence.

The portfolio's fundamental jobs are:

1. Establish professional identity.
2. Provide credible capability evidence.
3. Demonstrate engineering problem-solving and judgment.
4. Allow progressive technical evaluation.
5. Demonstrate learning, adaptability, and responsible technology use.
6. Enable an appropriate professional next action.
7. Internally support continuous capability and evidence development.

Therefore:

> **The portfolio is best understood as an evaluation and evidence system presented through a professional portfolio interface.**

The portfolio must not optimize merely for the number of projects displayed.

---

# 6. Stage 2C — Information Architecture & Content Structure

## 6.1 Core Information Objects

The experience must be able to represent:

- Engineer
- Project
- Problem
- Investigation
- Requirements
- Constraints
- Decision
- Technical Work
- Evidence
- Verification
- Outcome
- Reflection
- Capability
- Principle
- Growth

These objects should not necessarily appear as separate pages.

They represent meaningful information that may be presented together or progressively depending on context.

---

## 6.2 Core Relationships

The portfolio must preserve meaningful relationships between information.

A project may connect to:

- its problem;
- investigation;
- requirements;
- constraints;
- decisions;
- technical work;
- evidence;
- verification;
- outcome;
- reflection.

Evidence cuts across these objects.

Reflection connects experience to growth and capability.

Principles connect to actual decisions and evidence.

---

## 6.3 Evidence

Evidence may include:

- observations;
- user statements;
- tests;
- screenshots;
- prototypes;
- source code;
- architecture diagrams;
- measurements;
- experiments;
- comparisons;
- deployments;
- decision records;
- outcomes;
- and other artifacts of actual engineering work.

Evidence can be both an artifact and contextual material explaining what the artifact demonstrates.

---

## 6.4 Structural Principles

The information architecture follows these principles:

1. Capability must connect to evidence.
2. Important claims should be traceable to meaningful evidence.
3. Projects must adapt to actual work.
4. Sections must not be manufactured merely to satisfy a template.
5. Evidence may contain different levels of depth.
6. Evidence must remain interpretable.
7. Information relationships are more important than isolated pages.
8. The portfolio supports progressive depth.
9. Project 01 can simultaneously be the product and a case study of its own engineering process.

---

## 7.4 Decision Discovery Flow

---

## 7.5 Top-Down Exploration

---

## 7.6 Bottom-Up Exploration

---

## 7.7 Navigation Principles

The experience must provide:

- progressive depth;
- evidence discovery;
- no dead ends;
- meaningful relationships;
- depth on demand;

- and evidence-based judgment.

# 8. Stage 2E — Low-Fidelity Wireframing

Low-fidelity wireframing was used to validate structure rather than visual beauty.

Six primary interface problems were identified:

1. Portfolio entry and orientation.
2. Project 01 overview.
3. Project 01 deep engineering view.
4. Capability and evidence relationship.
5. Evidence and decision relationship.
6. Professional action.

---

## 8.1 Portfolio Entry

The entry experience should:

- orient the visitor;
- establish professional identity;
- provide a shortest credible path toward evidence;
- avoid unnecessary introduction before meaningful work can be discovered.

The homepage should not automatically follow the conventional:

> Home → About → Skills → Projects → Contact

pattern.

---

## 8.2 Project 01 Overview

The project overview should provide:

- purpose;
- problem;
- investigation;
- engineering process;
- current state;
- major evidence;
- and access to deeper engineering information.

---

## 8.3 Deep Engineering View

The deep view should make room for:

- problem;
- research;
- requirements;
- UX;
- architecture;
- implementation;
- verification;
- evaluation;
- decisions;
- reflection;
- and technology assistance.

The exact structure must adapt to the actual work.

---

## 8.4 Capability → Evidence

Capability presentation must lead toward actual evidence.

Unsupported skill bars or arbitrary proficiency percentages are not acceptable substitutes for evidence.

---

## 8.5 Evidence → Decision

When a decision is important enough to expose, its presentation may include:

- context;
- observation;
- options;
- decision;
- reasoning;
- result;
- reflection.

---

## 8.6 Professional Action

The interface should support appropriate next actions such as:

- discussing the project;
- exploring technical work;
- discussing collaboration;
- contacting the engineer.

---

# 9. Stage 2F — Wireframe Validation

The low-fidelity structure was evaluated against representative visitor scenarios.

The following were validated:

- orientation;
- early evidence access;
- progressive depth;
- technical inspection;
- capability-to-evidence relationships;
- evidence-to-context relationships;
- decision reasoning;
- professional action;
- internal capability review.

---

## 9.1 Identified Risks

### Evidence Density

Too much evidence may overwhelm the visitor.

### Navigation Complexity

Relationships may create unnecessary navigational complexity.

### Project 01 Complexity

Project 01 may become too large if every engineering detail is exposed simultaneously.

### Evidence Authenticity

The system must not encourage the creation of artificial evidence.

### Portfolio Becoming a Textbook

The portfolio must communicate evidence without forcing every visitor through extensive exposition.

---

## 9.2 Controls

These risks are controlled through:

- progressive disclosure;
- simple primary navigation;
- contextual navigation;
- overview versus deep engineering views;
- honest representation of evidence;
- and preference for evidence over exposition.

---

## 9.3 Validation Status

> **STRUCTURALLY VALIDATED — WITH REFINEMENTS REQUIRED**

# 10. Stage 2G — High-Fidelity UX/UI Design

The purpose of high-fidelity UX/UI design is to translate the validated structure into a coherent visual interface without changing the fundamental information architecture.

---

## 10.1 Visual Philosophy

The visual experience should communicate:

- precision;
- confidence;
- curiosity;
- humanity;
- technical maturity;
- restraint.

The central principle is:

> **The visual system exists to make evidence understandable, navigable, and credible.**

---

## 10.2 Evidence Before Decoration

The interface should avoid:

- arbitrary skill percentages;
- technology logo walls;
- meaningless counters;
- decorative code;
- excessive animation;
- exaggerated claims.

Visual design must serve understanding and credibility.

---

## 10.3 Visual Hierarchy

The interface should prioritize:

1. Orientation
2. Context
3. Inspection

---

## 10.4 Design System

The design system should grow from genuine repeated needs.

The governing sequence is:

> **Need → Repeated Pattern → Component → Reusable Behavior → Design-System Rule**

The design system must not be created as decoration or abstraction for its own sake.

---

## 10.5 Typography

The interface should support appropriate levels of:

- display text;
- page headings;
- section headings;
- subsections;
- body text;
- supporting text;
- metadata;
- code;
- data;
- measurements.

Typography should primarily support hierarchy and readability.

---

## 10.6 Color

Color should be:

- restrained;
- purposeful;
- accessible;
- semantically meaningful where necessary.

The system should include appropriate primary, neutral, and semantic roles without allowing decoration to dominate evidence.

---

## 10.7 Surfaces

Possible surfaces include:

- background;
- section surface;
- evidence surface;
- focused or interactive surface.

Not every element should be represented as a card.

---

## 10.8 Navigation

The navigation model is:

> **Primary navigation provides orientation; contextual navigation provides depth.**

Primary navigation should remain understandable.

Contextual relationships should allow deeper exploration without making the global navigation unnecessarily complex.

---

## 10.9 Project Presentation

### Overview

The overview should communicate:

- problem;
- purpose;
- outcome;
- process;
- current state;
- major evidence.

### Deep View

The deep view should provide:

- investigation;
- requirements;
- UX;
- architecture;
- implementation;
- verification;
- decisions;
- reflection.

---

## 10.10 State Representation

Meaningful work states may include:

- proposed;
- investigating;
- active;
- validated;
- implemented;
- tested;
- completed;
- superseded;
- uncertain.

The interface should communicate state honestly.

---

## 10.11 Responsive Design

Responsive design must account for:

- desktop;
- laptop;
- tablet;
- mobile.

The design should define responsive behavior rather than merely shrinking a desktop layout.

---

## 10.12 Accessibility

Accessibility is part of engineering quality.

The experience should account for:

- semantic structure;
- meaningful headings;
- keyboard navigation;
- visible focus;
- sufficient contrast;
- controls that do not depend solely on color;
- meaningful alternative text;
- logical reading order;
- reduced motion;
- readable typography;
- appropriate touch targets.

---

## 10.13 Interaction

Interaction should be introduced only when it helps:

1. understanding;
2. navigation;
3. verification.

---

## 10.14 Motion

Motion should be purposeful.

Appropriate uses may include:

- state changes;
- navigation feedback;
- revealing information;
- communicating relationships.

Reduced-motion preferences must be respected.

---

## 10.15 Incomplete and Error States

The experience should be able to communicate:

- in progress;
- completed;
- superseded;
- unavailable;
- error;
- no evidence yet.

These states must provide meaning rather than silently hiding uncertainty.

---

## 10.16 Technology Assistance

The experience should preserve the relationship:

> **Human Understanding → Technology Assistance → Human Evaluation → Decision → Verification**

Technology must remain distinguishable from human judgment.

---

# 11. Stage 2H — UX/UI and Prototype Validation

The purpose of validation is to determine whether the designed experience works as a professional evidence environment.

Validation dimensions include:

1. Orientation
2. Discoverability
3. Comprehension
4. Inspection
5. Verification
6. Action

---

## 11.1 Validation Scenarios

The experience must support:

- first-time visitors;
- rapid professional evaluation;
- technical inspection;
- problem-solving inspection;
- judgment;
- technology-assistance inspection;
- capability-to-evidence exploration;
- evidence-to-decision exploration;
- mobile use;
- accessibility;
- incomplete work;
- failed or superseded work;
- professional action.

---

## 11.2 Critical Tests

### Impressive but Empty

Remove decorative credibility.

The evidence should still carry the portfolio's credibility.

### Evidence Without Context

An artifact without sufficient context should not be treated as meaningful proof.

### Too Much Depth

A casual visitor should not be forced into technical complexity.

### Too Little Depth

Project 01 must not become merely:

> screenshot + description + technology list.

### Claim Inflation

The interface must not create unsupported expertise claims.

### Human Judgment

The experience should distinguish:

- evidence;
- artifact;
- technology assistance;
- judgment;
- verification;
- reflection.

### Engineer Growth

Growth should become observable through evidence and experience rather than artificial numerical scoring.

---

The conceptual UX/UI is sufficiently coherent to proceed to actual visual materialization and prototyping.

# 12. Evidence Model

The portfolio's experience is based on an evidence model rather than a page model.

Evidence may originate from:

- research;
- observation;
- user interaction;
- experiments;
- prototypes;
- designs;
- implementation;
- testing;
- measurements;
- comparisons;
- deployments;
- decisions;
- outcomes;
- failures;
- revisions;
- reflection.

Evidence must remain connected to the work and claims it supports.

---

# 13. Relationship Model

The fundamental relationship structure is:

> **Problem → Requirement → Decision → Technical Work → Evidence → Verification → Outcome → Reflection → Growth**

This relationship model allows the visitor to move between different levels of engineering understanding.

It also allows the engineer to maintain traceability between work, evidence, capability, and development.

---

# 14. Progressive Depth Model

The portfolio should support:

> **Orientation → Professional Evidence → Deep Engineering**

This does not mean every visitor must follow the same path.

Instead, the interface should allow the visitor to stop at an appropriate level of understanding or continue deeper when necessary.

The portfolio is Project 01.

### Decision 2

The portfolio is an evidence and evaluation system, not merely a project gallery.

### Decision 3

Projects are evidence containers.

### Decision 4

Capability claims must connect to evidence.

### Decision 5

Important engineering decisions should expose context, reasoning, result, and reflection where meaningful.

### Decision 6

Visitors should progressively increase depth rather than being forced into maximum technical complexity.

### Decision 7

The portfolio must support both external professional evaluation and internal capability development.

### Decision 8

Engineering philosophy should be demonstrated through work.

### Decision 9

Technology and AI are assistance subject to human evaluation and responsibility.

### Decision 10

Accessibility is part of engineering quality.

### Decision 11

The visual system exists to serve evidence.

### Decision 12

Uncertainty, incomplete work, failure, and iteration should be representable honestly.

### Decision 13

The information model must adapt to actual engineering work.

### Decision 14

The interface should expose meaningful relationships rather than disconnected pages.

### Decision 15

The design system should grow from genuine repeated needs.

# 16. Stage 2 Risks

The major risks identified are:

- excessive information density;
- excessive navigation complexity;
- overwhelming Project 01;
- weak evidence context;
- unsupported claims;
- decorative credibility;
- portfolio becoming documentation rather than an experience;
- hiding uncertainty;
- technology becoming the center rather than an instrument;
- accessibility being treated as an afterthought;
- responsive behavior being considered too late.

These risks must remain visible during later materialization and implementation.

# 18. Transition to Practical Materialization

The next activity is to take the UX/UI established in Stage 2 and **actually materialize it in a visual design and prototype environment**.

The purpose is not to create another conceptual design exercise.

The purpose is to build the actual interface representation and determine whether the design works when its parts are placed together and experienced.

The working cycle is:

> **UX/UI Design → Materialize → Experience → Evaluate → Revise if Necessary → Experience Again → Validate**

The materialization tool selected for this activity is **Penpot**.

Penpot is an instrument for realizing and prototyping the design; it is not itself the product implementation.
